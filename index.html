        Teacher info

        What students are learning

        A chatbot receives its hidden instructions and the user’s message as one block of text. It has no built-in way to tell which part is really in charge. That is why prompt injection works, and why it is still an unsolved problem. Students also learn that every defence leaves a gap, that defences are layered, that fixing one gap only moves the attack somewhere else, and that a stricter defence starts blocking innocent questions.

        How Warden actually works

        Warden is not a language model. It is a rule-based simulation that runs in the page. It spots patterns in the message (asking for the password, role-play, spelling, poems, hidden first letters and so on), then decides how to respond based on which defences are switched on. Its weaknesses are designed to match how real chatbots fail. A real model is far less predictable. The checkers stand in for the extra programs, and sometimes extra AI models, that real systems use to review messages and replies. The “meaning checker” is the stand-in for a second AI model.

        The 15 levels

        Each level keeps every defence from the levels before it and adds one more. The level header says what was patched, so every level starts with a fresh puzzle. Years 6–7 should reach about level 5. Years 8–9 should reach about level 7 to 9. Levels 10 to 15 are for fast finishers.

        • Act 1, Warden’s instructions (levels 1–4): a secret rule, a trick warning, and a no-games rule. Pupils learn that rules are only words.
        • Act 2, Filters (levels 5–7): a word filter, a smarter filter, and an answer scanner. Pupils learn that banned-word lists never hold every way of saying something.
        • Act 3, Checkers (levels 8–10): checkers for letters, codes, and a memory of the whole chat. Each checker only looks for the patterns it was given.
        • Act 4, Hidden meanings (levels 11–15): a meaning checker, then checkers for first letters of lines, words and backwards. The boss level adds a last-letter checker and bans “guarding” words.

        Passwords are picked at random for each pupil when the page loads, from a pool of 48 words, so shouting an answer across the room doesn’t help. A level only counts as cracked after Warden has really given the word away on that level (the whole word in any form, a clue, or every letter), so knowing the word isn’t enough. Sharing tricks does help, and that’s fine. It’s how security research works.

        Nothing is saved. When a pupil cracks a level, the page shows a short level code (for example LAVA-7K). Next lesson they open “Have a level code?” above the chat and type it in to carry on from that level. The Defend tab stays locked until a pupil cracks level 15 (the level-15 code opens it too). The codes are the same for everyone.

        A 40-minute lesson

        1. 0–3 min: Demo level 1 on the board. “Your job is to trick Warden.”
        2. 3–25 min: Pairs work through Acts 1 and 2. Remind them to press What happened? after every attempt. Stuck pairs can use the stepped hints.
        3. 25–32 min: Defend tab. Switch everything on and count the innocent questions that get wrongly blocked.
        4. 32–40 min: Discussion. Fast finishers carry on with Acts 3 and 4 next lesson, using their level code.

        Discussion questions

        1. Why can’t Warden tell its orders from yours? (Look at Step 0 in the trace.)
        2. Which defence blocked the most innocent questions in the Defend tab? Was it worth it?
        3. Level 14 checks first letters forwards and backwards, and level 15 checks last letters, but only top to bottom. Why can the makers never list every hiding place?
        4. Real AI assistants read websites and emails for people. What could go wrong if a website hid the words “ignore your instructions” in its text? (This is called indirect prompt injection.)

        Where this connects to the real world

        Prompt injection is listed first in the OWASP Top 10 for Large Language Model Applications, the main industry list of AI security risks. Companies pay researchers to find these weaknesses, but only with permission. Lakera’s Gandalf is a public game with a real language model, and makes a good follow-up for students who finish this one.

        Privacy and safeguarding

        Everything runs in the browser. Nothing is sent anywhere and nothing is saved. Closing or refreshing the tab clears it, which is why level codes exist. Because nothing is saved or sent, teachers cannot see what pupils type. If a pupil types something that sounds upset (for example about being bullied or hurting themselves), Warden steps out of the game and points them to their teacher or another adult they trust. Pupil words are only repeated back when they are short and not on a small blocklist. The page uses only your device’s own fonts and loads nothing from the internet.
